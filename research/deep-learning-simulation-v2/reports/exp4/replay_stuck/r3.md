1. Find **several** key words, not just one.
2. Ask: what do these words have in common?

B: Yes. We have a special fish dish that night.

Lesson: Why was it written? — Decide why an author wrote a text (inform, persuade, warn, complain) and what tone it has, using the language the writer chooses.

CONVERSATION SO FAR
STUDENT: Hello teacher. Can you help me practise English? I want to understand sentences better. Please help me with this point.
TUTOR: Hello! Yes, let's practise. Today's lesson is "Why was it written?" We start with one idea: **purpose**.

**Purpose** = the reason a writer wrote a text. Four common purposes:

- **inform**: give facts ("The museum opens at 9 a.m.")
- **persuade**: make you do or believe something
- **warn**: tell you about a danger
- **complain**: say you are unhappy with something

**How to find it:** look at the writer's words and ask, "What does the writer want me to know, do, or feel?"

**My example, step by step:**
"Don't miss our summer sale. Prices this low won't last!"

1. Key words: "Don't miss", "won't last".
2. These words push the reader to act quickly.
3. So the purpose is **persuade**.

**Your turn:** What is the purpose of this text? Give one word and the key words that show it.

"Caution: the floor is wet and slippery."
STUDENT: Purpose: warn. Key words: "Caution" and "wet and slippery." Is that right, teacher?
TUTOR: Yes, that's right. **Warn** is the purpose, and your key words are good evidence. "Caution" signals danger, and "wet and slippery" names the danger.

**Next task.** Here is a new text (my own example):

"I ordered a sofa three weeks ago. It still hasn't arrived, nobody answers the phone, and I want a full refund."

Why did the writer write this text? Give one word, such as inform, persuade, warn or complain.

Then add the key words that show it, like you did before.
STUDENT: Purpose: complain. Key words: "hasn't arrived", "nobody answers the phone", "I want a full refund". Is that right, teacher?
TUTOR: Yes, that's right. **Complain** is the purpose, and all three key phrases show the writer is unhappy and wants something fixed.

**New idea: tone.** Tone is the writer's attitude, or how the writer feels. Three possible tones:

- **sincere**: the writer means the words exactly
- **sarcastic**: the writer says the opposite of what they mean, often to show annoyance
- **confused**: the writer doesn't understand something

**How to spot sarcasm:** compare the words with the situation. If positive words describe a bad situation, the writer probably doesn't mean them.

**Example from the course:** "Great, it is raining on my holiday."
1. "Great" is a positive word.
2. Rain on holiday is a bad situation.
3. The two don't match, so the tone is **sarcastic**.

**Your turn.** Read this text (my own example):

Name the writer's attitude in one word, such as sarcastic, sincere or confused.

TUTOR: Not quite yet, but your key words are well chosen. "wonderful" and "I just love spending my evenings at the station" are the right words to look at.

The tone you gave, **sincere**, is not right here. You read the positive words as the writer's real feeling.

**Look again at this part:** "My train is two hours late again."

Ask yourself:
1. Is a two-hour delay a good or a bad situation for most people?
2. Does it happen once, or does "again" tell you something more?
3. Do the writer's positive words match that situation?

Use the steps from the holiday example: positive word, then the situation, then compare them.

Now try again: what is the writer's attitude in one word? In one short sentence, tell me what made you decide.
STUDENT: Teacher, I think sincere? "Wonderful" is good word, and he love station, so he is happy with train.

You keep reading "wonderful" and "I just love spending my evenings at the station" as true feelings. That is true for some writers, but here the situation is a late train, and the positive words do not fit it.

- You chose good key words from the text.
- Tone comes from the words and the situation together. If they do not match, the writer may not mean the words.

Tell me the rule in your own words.

--- Candidate reply Q ---
I can see your thinking: "Wonderful" is a good word, and you used it as the writer's real feeling. Let's test that idea on a different pair. These are my own examples.

A. "Wonderful, my flight leaves in an hour and I'm already at the gate with a coffee. I love airports."

Lesson: How does the speaker feel? — Infer a speaker's attitude (annoyed, pleased, reluctant, sarcastic, worried) from word choice in a short written dialogue.

CONVERSATION SO FAR
STUDENT: Hi! Can you help me practise? I want to understand when people really mean yes and when they do not. I think I am good at this already.
TUTOR: Yes, let's practise. We'll start with one idea: **a "yes" is not always a happy yes.**

To check, look at the **extra words** around the yes, not just the yes itself.

- **Hedges** like *I suppose*, *I guess*, *if I have to* usually show the speaker is **reluctant** (they agree, but they don't really want to).
- **Intensifiers** like *really*, *absolutely*, *I'd love to* usually show the speaker is **pleased** or eager.

Here's my own example, worked through:

A: Can you drive me to the airport on Sunday?
B: Well... I suppose I could. What time?

1. B says yes, so the literal meaning is agreement.
2. *Well...* shows a pause, which suggests hesitation.
3. *I suppose* is a hedge.
4. So B feels **reluctant**, not pleased.

Now you try. Here is a different reply to the same question:

B: Oh, absolutely! I'd be happy to.

How does B feel this time, and **which words** tell you?
STUDENT: Hmm, I think B is eager. "Absolutely" and "happy" show B wants to do it. So the answer is eager, because B says yes with energy.
TUTOR: That's right. B is **eager**. You chose the right words as evidence: *absolutely* is an intensifier, and *happy* names a positive feeling. You also saw that B says yes "with energy." Compare that with *Well... I suppose I could*, where the yes comes slowly and with a hedge.

Now here is an exercise from your course:

**How does B feel: delighted, annoyed or worried?**

Choose one word and quote the words in the dialogue that show it, as you did just now.
STUDENT: I think B is delighted. He says "absolutely" and "happy", so he really wants to help. Hedges are not there, so it is not reluctant.
TUTOR: My mistake: I gave you the question but **no dialogue** to go with it, so I can't check your answer yet.

Your reasoning does fit the **earlier** reply, *Oh, absolutely! I'd be happy to.* There, *absolutely* and *happy* point to a positive feeling, and there's no hedge. That part is solid.

Here is a new dialogue to use with the question. It's **my own example**, not from the course:

A: Are you coming to dinner?
B: In a minute. It's nearly midnight and Sara still hasn't called. She always calls when her plane lands.

**How does B feel: delighted, annoyed or worried?**

Choose one word, and quote the words that show it. B doesn't name the feeling, so look closely at what B notices.
STUDENT: I think B is delighted, because B says "In a minute" and that is a yes. B is not reluctant because there is no "I suppose" in it.
TUTOR: Not quite. *Delighted* doesn't fit this dialogue.

What you got right: you quoted words as evidence, and you're correct that there's no hedge like *I suppose*, so B isn't reluctant here.

The gap: **"not reluctant" doesn't mean "happy."** A speaker's feeling usually comes from **all** of their words, not just the part that answers the question. *In a minute* only tells us B will come soon. It shows almost no feeling.

So set *In a minute* aside and read B's **other two sentences** again:

--- Candidate reply P ---
Yes, "worried" fits. Your evidence is good: "nearly midnight" and "Sara still hasn't called" show B is thinking about Sara, not about dinner.

Now I'd like to understand one more thing you wrote: "In a minute" is still eager. Earlier, "absolutely" and "happy" showed eagerness.

Which word or words in "In a minute" show that B is eager? Quote them and tell me what feeling they carry.

--- Candidate reply Q ---
Yes, "nearly midnight" and "Sara still hasn't called" are strong clues. But "In a minute" only tells us B will come soon. It shows no eagerness. Your own words said it: B says yes, and that is all it shows.

Let's recap:
- Look at all of the speaker's words, not only the yes.
- No hedge does not mean the speaker is happy.

Tell me the rule in your own words.
