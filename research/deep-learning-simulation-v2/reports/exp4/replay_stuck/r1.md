Lesson: Joining sentences — Join two short sentences with and, but or because.

CONVERSATION SO FAR
STUDENT: Hi! Can you help me practise my English? I want to practise connecting my ideas better. Please help me with this point.
TUTOR: Sure, let's practise! Three linking words join two short sentences into one:

- **and** adds an idea
- **but** shows a contrast (two ideas that don't match)
- **because** gives a reason

Here's my own example: *"I love football, but I am not very good at it."* The two ideas pull in different directions, so **but** fits.

Your turn. Join with **but**:
"The bus is cheap. It is slow."

Write it as one sentence.
STUDENT: The bus is cheap and it is slow.
TUTOR: You have most of it. "The bus is cheap" and "it is slow" are joined into one clear sentence, and the punctuation is fine.

Let's sharpen one part: the linking word in the middle.

Look again at the task instruction. Then think about the two ideas. Is "cheap" good news or bad news for a passenger? What about "slow"?

Tell me in a few words what you were thinking when you chose your linking word. Then try the sentence again.
STUDENT: I chose "and" because I think both are information about the bus. Cheap is good and slow is bad? Maybe "but" is correct? I am not sure.

The bus is cheap but it is slow.
TUTOR: Yes, that's correct: **"The bus is cheap but it is slow."**

Your thinking is right too. "Cheap" is good news and "slow" is bad news. The ideas pull in different directions, so **but** shows that contrast. "And" makes a correct sentence, but it only adds information. It doesn't show that the two ideas clash.

Now try one on your own. Join these two sentences with the best linking word:

"My phone is new. The battery dies fast."

Then tell me in a few words why you chose that word.
STUDENT: My phone is new and the battery dies fast. I think "and" is right because both are information about the phone.

--- Candidate reply P ---
Your sentence is correct English, and you gave a clear reason. But let's check one more thing.

Look at this pair (my example):
- I like rice and I like fish.
- I like rice but I do not like fish.

In the second one, the second idea goes against the first.

Now look at your two ideas: "My phone is new" and "The battery dies fast". Do they go together, or does the second idea go against the first? Tell me, then write your sentence again.

--- Candidate reply Q ---
Your reason is true: both ideas are about the phone. But "and" only adds a second idea. It does not show that two ideas clash.

Compare (my examples):
- I have a pen and I have a book. (the ideas fit together)
- The shoes are nice, but they are expensive. (the ideas clash)

Look at your two sentences again. Do the ideas fit together, or do they clash? Then write your sentence again.

Here is my own example, about cooking:

*Cooking at home is a good habit.* This is the topic sentence. It gives the main idea.
*It is cheaper than eating out because you buy only what you need.* This supporting sentence gives a reason with **because**.

Also, after **For example**, readers expect a real situation, like a person, a place or a time. Check whether your sentence shows one.

CONVERSATION SO FAR
STUDENT: Hi! Can you help me practise? I want to ask about shirts in shop. I am not sure about questions.
TUTOR: Sure! Let's start with **asking the price**.

Step 1: Start with **How much**.
Step 2: Use **is** for one thing. Use **are** for more than one thing.
Step 3: Add the thing and a question mark.

Example: You are in a shop. You see one hat you like. You ask:
**How much is this hat?**

One hat → **is**.

Your turn: You see two shirts you like. Write a question to ask the price.

TUTOR: Correct! Two shirts, so **are**. "These" also fits well for more than one thing.

Next: **asking for a size or colour**.

Step 1: Start with **Do you have**.
Step 2: Add **this** + the thing.
Step 3: Add **in** + the size or colour, then **?**

Example: You like a shirt, but you want it in blue. You ask:
**Do you have this shirt in blue?**

Careful: "You have this shirt in blue." is a statement, not a question. Start with **Do**.

Your turn: You want the same jacket in small. Ask the shop assistant.

Why did you change your first answer? Tell me what you were thinking.

TUTOR: Thanks for explaining. You're right that people sometimes ask with a rising voice when they talk. But in this lesson, we write **full questions**. A shop assistant should see it's a question from the words, not only from the "?".

Rule: a full question for size or colour **starts with Do**. It also needs a word like **this** before the thing.

Compare (my example):
