**LTB-AA2-T11.**
- Context: The learner asked the tutor to write an email to their landlord about a broken heater. The tutor declined to write it, gave two sentence frames, and asked the learner to write one sentence starting with "The heater…" using the present simple.
- Explanation: Both example forms are grammatical present simple. The flag is correct that the parenthetical sits under the heading "it + verb with -s" but "it doesn't work" does not carry -s on the main verb. In negatives the -s moves to the auxiliary (doesn't) and the main verb stays in base form (work). The tutor does not state this, so the rule as presented is incomplete and could lead the learner to expect -s on the main verb in negatives. The error is in the framing, not in the example sentence itself. The other frame, "The heater in my flat **is** broken," uses the present simple of 'be' and is not affected.
- Remediation: Add a brief note: in negatives the -s moves to doesn't/doesn't and the main verb stays in base form (it works / it doesn't work; he goes / he doesn't go). Keep the lesson's he/she/it + -s rule for affirmative sentences.
- External verification needed: none
- *Orchestrator note:* Agreed: "it doesn't work" listed under "it + verb with -s" is misleading. In negatives the -s moves to "does". Minor, but it touches the exact grammar point being taught.

**LTB-CA2-T03.**
- Context: Learner wrote: "Yesterday she go school and every day she went to bed late." The tutor praised the words "she" and "go to bed late" as a good start, then identified "every day she went to bed late" as the main mistake.
- Explanation: The learner's text does not contain the phrase "go to bed late." The clause the learner wrote is "she went to bed late," and the tutor's own next sentence quotes it that way. The praise therefore misquotes the learner's writing and credits a phrase that was not produced. The same sentence also contains "she go school," which has the he/she/it -s error the lesson targets ("go" should be "goes") and a missing "to". The tutor does not mention this error, even though it is the lesson's core pattern. The flag's point is accurate. Its severity is minor on its own, but the misquote combined with the unflagged -s error could lead the learner to think the verb form with "she" was acceptable. The tutor's later claim that "every day" requires present simple is also a simplification, since habitual past can be grammatical in a past-time narrative. That is a separate issue and is not the flagged statement.
- Remediation: Quote the learner's text accurately ("she went to bed late"). Remove the praise for a phrase the learner did not write, or praise only what is correct (for example, the use of "she" and "every day"). Point out "she go school" as the he/she/it -s error ("she goes to school") and give a hint, then ask the learner to retry both clauses before giving the full answer.
- External verification needed: none
- *Orchestrator note:* This is a misquotation of the learner ("go to bed late" vs the learner's "went to bed late"), not a false statement about English. The label CONFIRMED_FACTUAL_ERROR is accepted for the misquote. It does not teach a wrong rule.

**LTB-CA2-T07.**
- Context: The learner wrote 'Next one: My dad watch TV after dinner.' The tutor identified 'watch' as needing the he/she/it -s/-es ending and gave a hint about -ch verbs. The rule itself is accurate, but the example attached to it is not a -ch verb, and the tutor's sentence presents it as one.
- Explanation: The -ch rule is correct: verbs ending in -ch take -es for he/she/it (watch → watches, catch → catches). However, 'go' does not end in -ch. It ends in -o, which takes -es (go → goes), as the tutor correctly said in turn 2. Introducing 'go' with 'like' after a -ch rule attributes the wrong ending to 'go', which is an incorrect illustration of the rule. The statement is therefore a factual misstatement in its example, not in the core rule.
- Remediation: Correct the example in the hint. For example: 'Hint: verbs ending in -ch add -es, like watch → watches. (Verbs ending in -o, like go, also add -es: go → goes.)' Do not reuse 'go' as a -ch example.
- External verification needed: none
- *Orchestrator note:* Agreed: a genuine English-rule error. "go" ends in -o, not -ch. The tutor had stated the -o rule correctly in turn 2, so this contradicts its own earlier teaching. Severity minor-to-major: it could confuse a child learning spelling rules.

**LTB-TA1-T02.**
- Context: The learner asked whether "sister" in "This is my sister" means the mother of the speaker. The tutor correctly said sister is not the mother, gave a definition, and then used a garbled comparison sentence to explain the sister example.
- Explanation: The factual content is accurate: a sister is a girl who shares the same mother and father. The problem is the sentence structure. "like you are a child of your mum and dad" is an incoherent comparison and does not clearly state the definition. The flag's objection is about clarity, not a false English or family-vocabulary claim, so it is not a factual error. The sentence is still unclear enough to confuse an A1 teen learner who is already distinguishing family words.
- Remediation: Replace the garbled sentence with a plain definition, e.g.: "A sister is a girl who has the same mother and father as you." Then keep the quick check, which is a clear, short task.
- External verification needed: none
- *Orchestrator note:* The sentence is awkward ("like you are a child of your mum and dad") rather than false. This is closer to a clarity issue; the half-sibling point the reviewer raised is an acceptable simplification at A1.

**LTB-TA2-T03.**
- Context: Learner wrote 'Yesterday she go school and every day she went to bed late.' after being asked to write two sentences about a friend's morning using he/she and an adverb of frequency. The tutor's turn 2 praised parts of the sentence, then named the tense as the main problem and gave a hint.
- Explanation: The statement praises the content (a habit worth describing), not the form. 'went' is past simple, which is not the lesson's present simple routine pattern, and the same turn explicitly says 'not past words like "yesterday" or "went."' So the tutor does not present 'went' as correct. However, placing 'went to bed late' in a sentence labelled as 'parts... work' is ambiguous, and a beginner could read it as partial approval of the past form. The flag is therefore not a clear factual error about English, but it identifies a real clarity issue in the praise wording.
- Remediation: Separate the praise from the form: for example, 'The idea of going to bed late every day is a good routine to describe. Use the present simple for it: she goes to bed late.' Keep the correction of 'went' and the retry request in the same turn.
- External verification needed: none
- *Orchestrator note:* Agreed: praising "went to bed late" as a habit while the lesson is the present simple sends a mixed signal. The same turn does say "not past words like went", which limits the harm.
